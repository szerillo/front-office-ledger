# We Scored Every MLB Front Office Since 1998
### Introducing the Front Office Ledger: every trade, draft pick, signing, extension, and waiver claim, graded against expectation and attributed to the regime that made it

*Draft for publication · numbers as of November 2, 2026 (2026 season and postseason final; engine v5.9)*

Anyone who has played Out of the Park Baseball knows the Decision History screen: every move you ever made, with the wins it gained or lost printed next to it, forever. Real general managers have never had to look at that screen. Beat writers grade trades the week they happen and rarely revisit them; fans argue with anecdotes; the actual ledger, the thing that would settle the argument, has never existed in public.

So I built it. The ledger ingests every transaction from the MLB Stats API and the Retrosheet archive (282,000 rows), assembles them into decisions, values every player involved with an open, self-computed metric (Ledger WAR, built entirely from public data: batting, pitching, defense, framing, baserunning, park factors), and grades six channels against what each kind of move is supposed to return. Draft picks grade against the historical value of their slot. Trades grade from both seats over control windows, net of the salary that moves, discounted 10% a year. Free agency grades realized value against what a typical club gets for the same money in the same contract years, on a deal of the same size, from a book of about 3,600 real contracts, with deferral-heavy deals costed at CBT value. Extensions grade against the path they replaced: arbitration salaries in the seasons the club already controlled, the free-agent market in the seasons it bought. International signings grade against their bonus-pool class. Waiver claims grade against zero, the cleanest read on pro scouting there is. Player development is its own grade, marked to market from sixteen years of top-100 prospect lists. Every grade is clickable down to the individual decisions that produced it. Nothing is a vibe.

And it is not just the thirty sitting regimes. The same engine runs over 122 past regimes back to 1998 (interim GMs included), each over its actual window: the complete Billy Beane record, Brian Sabean's full twenty years, Theo Epstein in both cities, Brian Cashman from his real 1998 seat. Careers are finally comparable on identical accounting.

## The leaderboard

Among sitting regimes with at least three seasons, per season of tenure, the board is led by **Chris Antonetti** (Cleveland, +102 wins above expectation over eleven seasons, on the sport's thinnest payrolls: +41 in the draft, +24 in extensions, +17 in trades), **Peter Bendix** (Miami, +21 in three, almost all of it from the waiver wire), **Matt Arnold** (Milwaukee, +28 in four, +35 in trades, and now the 2026 World Series), **Chris Getz** (Chicago, +22 in three) and **Ross Atkins** (Toronto, +65 over eleven, with the best international record in the book at +32). At the bottom by total: **Brian Cashman** (the Yankees, -143 over 29 years) and **A.J. Preller** (San Diego, -90 over twelve).

One caution belongs right here, because the ledger measured it about itself: within a single regime, trade, draft and waiver results do not repeat. Split any front office's deals into alternating halves and the two halves are uncorrelated across clubs. Only free agency (and, faintly, extensions) shows a club doing the same thing well twice. So the letters are an accounting of results, what each decision returned against what a typical club gets from the same move. They are not a promise about the next one.

Some of the results confirm what everyone believes. The value of a ledger is what it says that nobody has been saying:

**The best draft room of the data era is Billy Beane's Oakland**, +76 wins over what the league got from the same slots in the same drafts, ahead of John Schuerholz's Atlanta (+66) and Tony Reagins's three Angels drafts (+65, one of them Mike Trout at 25th overall). Each pick is judged against its own class, so the early-2000s drafts are no longer penalized for an era in which every club's picks returned less. Among sitting regimes it is Antonetti's Cleveland at +41.

**The full Cashman window is the hardest grade in the book to argue with and the easiest to misread.** Over his real 29-year seat the trades are -75 and the drafts -50 against their slots, the long veteran extensions (Jeter, Posada, Rivera) -27 against what free-agent money typically buys, and the international program +15. The Yankees also have the best results grade in the sport. Both are true: the ledger prices decisions against what the same money and the same picks typically return, and the Yankees spent and picked more than anyone. The ledger's job is to put both numbers on the same row.

**The Elias gap is real, quantified, and the whole debate in one row.** Baltimore grades A- on decisions (+24 above slot in the draft room and +22 on the waiver wire, second only to Bendix among sitting regimes) and a D in results: one division title and no playoff series wins. Whether you weight the machine or the harvest is a values question.

**Preller's grade is a trade diagnosis.** San Diego's -80 trade channel is the whole story: the drafts grade above slot (+5), but the picks were converted into trade chips and the conversions did not earn it back (the Soto deal alone reads -16 for San Diego once Abrams, Gore and Wood are counted through their control years).

**The best extension in the book is Detroit's 2008 Miguel Cabrera deal**, +27 wins against what that money typically buys, three months after the trade that brought him. The worst is the same player's 2014 extension (-23). Cleveland's two Jose Ramirez deals (2017 and 2022) are +11 and +16. Atlanta's famous early-career extensions grade only mildly positive, because each class is judged against what that kind of extension typically returns: early-career deals are team-friendly by design, so the skill is picking the right player.

**The rental market is cheaper than the anguish.** From 224 deadline rentals classified exactly from the league feed: a rental win costs about 0.8 wins of eventually-realized value (the nine true star rentals ran nearer 1.2, too few to call), the median deadline rental returns approximately nothing, and more than half of rental buys deliver under 0.3 wins. Boston's return for one expected year of Mookie Betts was market rate. The criticism was never the price; it was choosing to sell that asset at all.

**How championships actually get built, measured across all 29 champions since 1998:** free agency has been the majority channel exactly once (the 2009 Yankees). The median title core was 48 percent built by its sitting regime in 1998-2009 and 74 percent in 2010-2026, so the trend toward self-built winners is real. But the handed-down title has not gone away: seven of the seventeen champions since 2010 won on majority-inherited cores, including three in a row (the 2021 Braves, the 2022 Astros, and the 2023 Rangers, 85 percent Jon Daniels' roster). An earlier build of this project read the trend as the inherited championship going extinct. Better roster accounting said otherwise, so the claim came down.

**The system audits its own inputs.** Tracing the 2009 Yankees' roster exposed that the MLB transaction feed is effectively empty before 2009. Every public analysis built on that feed shares the hole. The fix was joining the Retrosheet transaction archive, 22,697 rows mapped to modern player ids, which put Beckett-for-Hanley and the Cabrera heist into the book, swung Epstein's Boston trade ledger by nearly fifty wins, and left the validation studies unchanged. Finding the gap was the system working. It happened again while building the scenario simulator: a decision scenario about the most famous extension in the book came back reading zero, because the player-value universe had been built from transaction-touched players, and a career-one-club star who never hits free agency touches almost no transactions. Jose Altuve, Jose Ramirez, Salvador Perez, Ronald Acuna Jr. and 129 others were invisible to the ledger. Restoring them improved the accounting validation to r = 0.86 and put Salvador Perez back on the 2015 Royals' championship core, where he had always belonged. Both corrections are documented in the methodology, with the numbers they moved.

## Does any of this mean anything?

Two tests, both reproducible from the repository with no licensed data. The accounting check: a WAR-class metric must sum to team record, and roster-summed Ledger WAR hits r = 0.86 against actual wins (mean error ~5 wins) across 327 team-seasons. The predictive check asks whether a regime's decision record, counted only as it had been realized by each season, predicts the next two seasons' winning percentage beyond the record to date. It barely does: across 576 regime-years from 112 regimes the partial correlation is about 0.1, with a cluster-bootstrap interval that includes zero, and it adds nothing out of sample under grouped cross-validation. An earlier version of this article reported 0.27; that figure let later seasons leak into the predictor, and the corrected result is the one that stands. The grades measure what decisions returned against what was expected. They are an accounting of results, not a forecast of next year's standings.

## The forward half

Accounting for the past was phase one. The same machinery now runs forward. A self-built projection layer (recent form regressed and walked forward on aging curves computed from the project's own 28,000-player cache, with a playing-time durability factor) is backtested at r = 0.62 to 0.67 against actual next-season WAR, competitive with public systems, and strictly quarantined: grades never move on a projection (the only expectation inside a grade is the one a deal was made on, such as a prospect's rank at the time, fading as his real seasons arrive).

That projection layer powers a **Deal Desk**: propose any trade, signing, or extension and get a verdict assembled from the ledger's own parts, including the market rate for deals of that shape, the nearest real comps and how they ended, and a counterparty card on the GM across the table. For contracts it does the work first: pick a player and it proposes the terms, a calibrated offer blending market comps and the model's fair price, backtested winter by winter against 1,196 real signings from 2010 to 2025 (typical miss: 0.7 years and $3.2M of annual value, with no systematic bias on the $200M-plus deals). Every verdict shows the club's estimated payroll before and after the move against the tax line, a Horizon view walks every roster forward through 2031 to show where the contention window actually sits, and any verdict exports as a one-page deal memo. The **Offseason Simulator** puts all of it in one chair: pick any club and run its real 2026-27 winter, from option and tender decisions through free agency and trades, against a camp that counters in its own years and refuses deals that do not make sense for it. An assistant GM reads the numbers back to you: which arbitration-eligible players to non-tender, what a fair offer looks like, how the closest real contracts aged, and which pieces in your own system bridge a short trade offer. The forward salary book, depth chart, and a closing grade against the five most similar real winters since 2009 come with it. Under the hood, a Statcast fingerprint layer reads what each front office actually shops for (contact, power, chase discipline, velocity, ground balls) from the profiles of the players it acquired, so the counterparty card on any deal can tell you what the other GM tends to want.

Pointed at internal projections and pref lists instead of public ones, this is a deadline war room. That swap is architectural, not a rebuild.

## What the grades are, and aren't

Everything here is versioned methodology, not gospel. Defense coverage thins before 2016. Extension class discounts are documented assumptions pending a larger book. Arb salaries in the payroll view are estimates and say so. Grades move when the methodology improves, every version is archived, and the biggest single correction in the project's history (the pre-2009 feed gap) is documented on the site rather than patched quietly. The honest disclosure that matters most: this measures front-office output against expectation, not executive souls. Budgets, owners, and luck all live inside these numbers. That is why every regime carries three grades, decisions, development, and results, and why the gaps between them are usually the most interesting thing on the page.

The ledger is live, every number is clickable, and the receipts go back to 1998. Every player's transaction tree pops out from wherever his name appears, so any move can be followed to where it ended. Argue with it. That is what it is for.
