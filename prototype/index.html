Methodology (abridged)

The unit of account is the decision: a dated transaction attributed to the executive regime in office when it was made. Each decision is scored in WAR captured (production received during the club-control window acquired) and surplus dollars (WAR × market $/WAR, minus salary paid). Departing players are scored symmetrically, so every deal nets to one number.

Grading against expectation, not zero

Draft picks are measured against the historical expected value of their slot; the #1 overall pick carries a far higher bar than pick #38. Prospects moved in trades are valued at the deal date using published prospect-tier surplus curves, so the ledger can score a trade both as it looked at the time and as it turned out; the gap between the two is the luck-versus-skill diagnostic. Free agency is graded as return on the market price of the contract, and the waiver/minor-league channel is graded against a baseline of zero: pure found money, the cleanest read on pro scouting. Net-of-cost is the universal display rule wherever a contract exists: FA signings and extensions never show raw WAR accumulation, because signing a star who then plays like a star is a pass, not a triumph.

Lenses, and open vs. closed

Every substantial decision is shown through a stack of named lenses rather than one verdict: windows-only (the control years actually exchanged), option-inclusive (adding retention rights and their exercise), and the counterfactual (this regime against its own realistic alternative), with raw v0.1 and leverage-adjusted v0.2 readings where they differ. Perspective rule: every lens on a regime's card is signed from that regime's seat. The other side of the same deal grades on the other regime's card, from theirs; the Betts trade reads as Friedman's win on Friedman's card and as Bloom's defensible sale on Boston's, and both are correct because each is measured against its own alternative. Alongside the lenses, every decision and every branch of a transaction tree carries a status: CLOSED means the window is finished and the number is final; OPEN means value is still accruing and the verdict can move. The site never averages the lenses into one number; the disagreement between them is the analysis.

Transaction trees

Assets compound across decisions: a fourth-round pick becomes a rental ace becomes a comp pick becomes a new draftee. The Trees view follows these chains generation by generation, marking each branch open or closed, with captured value in solid cards and outflows in dashed ones. Trees are also the honest way to display sell-offs: the Orioles' 2025 deadline reads as a dozen open branches, not a graded verdict.

Win-curve leverage & time discounting (v0.2)

A marginal win is worth 3–4× more at 89 projected wins than at 70 or 105, so acquired WAR is multiplied by a leverage curve, L(w) = 0.5 + 1.3·e−(w−89)²/200, peaking at 1.80, evaluated at the acquirer's projected wins on the decision date: the front office is graded on the leverage it thought it was buying. Future-year value discounts to the decision date at 10%/yr (6–14% sensitivity shown): a 2029 prospect win was never worth a 2024 pennant-race win to a defending champion, and win-now is a rational preference the ledger must price, not a bias to correct. Together these fixed v0.1's systematic bias (deadline buys punished, sell-side hauls flattered) and flipped the Burnes trade from "worst decision of the tenure" (−2.9) to a fair playoff-push price (+1.2). Every parameter (curve shape, discount rate, projected wins per case) is versioned and public.

Retention rights & opportunity cost

A trade transfers a third asset beyond the control windows: the exclusive right to extend the player. Pricing it at zero produces indefensible verdicts: the windows-only ledger says Boston "won" the Betts trade. Every trade therefore shows three ledgers: windows-only; option-inclusive (realized extension surplus flows to the acquirer once the right is exercised, the headline number); and the seller's counterfactual, charged p(retain) × forfeited extension surplus, where p is a documented, versioned judgment drawn from negotiation reporting. The same machinery scores passive retention failures (letting a star walk is charged p-weighted forfeited surplus with the comp pick credited), so "traded him with team control left" and "let him leave for nothing" become directly comparable. Under this accounting deals stop being zero-sum: in the Betts case both front offices beat their realistic alternatives, which is the honest and more interesting answer.

Attribution rules

A regime is graded only on its own decisions; inherited players are excluded. Extensions, non-tenders, and passive losses (a free agent walking, a player lost on waivers) are decisions too. Recent regimes carry a small-sample badge rather than being hidden, and the leaderboard defaults to realized value only, with projected-remaining value shown separately. Every rating is clickable down to the individual decisions that produced it, and every methodology version is archived so grades are reproducible. The two Betts decisions on the Dodgers card are scored with real data as a proof of concept; the trade and the extension are separate, chainable decisions, and that one example shows why: on windows alone the deal reads as an overpay, while the full chain reads as one of the great decisions of the era. Same inputs, different lenses, both on the page.

Two grades, on purpose

FOR (Front Office Rating) measures decision quality: value added versus expectation across the six channels. Team Success measures on-field results during the tenure: winning percentage, division titles, playoff appearances, playoff series wins, pennants, and World Series. They are deliberately separate: a front office can out-decide the league and still miss Octobers (payroll, luck, timing), or ride inherited stars to banners. The gap between the two grades is often the most interesting number on the page. Grades display as letters (A+ through F) mapped from the underlying 20–80 scouting scale, which stays available on hover.

Historical depth

The ledger is designed to backfill decade by decade; transactions exist back to the 1870s (Retrosheet), so historical regimes (Weiss, Rickey, Schuerholz, Gillick) sit on the same scale as active ones. Channel definitions adapt by era: before the 1965 amateur draft, the "draft" channel reads as amateur and international signings. Era-specific $/WAR baselines keep cross-era comparisons honest.
